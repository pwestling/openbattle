OpenBattle design · Level 0 · Intent

OpenBattle

What it is for

Two people who own miniatures armies play a full game of their tabletop wargame together online, each in their own browser, with nothing to install.

For whom

Porter and the friends he plays with: people who already know their game's rules and want a shared table, not a referee.

How we would know it worked

Two players on different networks finish a full game of one real wargame using only OpenBattle for the table, the tape measure and the dice.

Open Which game is the yardstick? Porter to name it.

What a player can do

  1. Open a new table and get a link to send to the other player.
  2. Join from the link, with a name, and see the same table.
  3. Place terrain: named shapes with a height.
  4. Bring an army by typing or pasting a list of units, model counts and base sizes.
  5. Move a model or a whole unit, and see the distance in inches while dragging.
  6. Measure between any two points or models.
  7. Roll dice that both players see at the same moment.
  8. Keep counters: wounds, victory points, the round.
  9. Read every action in a shared log, scrub back through it, and undo the latest action.
  10. Close the tab, come back later from the same link, and carry on. Save the game to a file.

The players apply the rules

The app enforces no game's rules and never refuses a move. It handles the physical parts of the table; the players handle the rest, as they would at home.

One table, seen the same way

Once both browsers have caught up, both players see exactly the same table. If they ever disagree, the app says so instead of quietly picking one.

Games survive

A game survives closing the tab, reloading, and either player's connection dropping.

Browser to browser

The players' browsers talk directly. Porter's Craftworld server hands out the app, introduces the browsers to each other, and relays their traffic only when their networks allow nothing else. It never stores a game.

No publisher content

OpenBattle ships no unit names, stats, rules text, art or models from any game publisher. Players bring their own.

Not in the first version

  • Automating any game's rules, or loading rules as add-ons
  • A 3D table, or line-of-sight checks
  • More than two players, spectators, or computer opponents
  • Hidden information such as cards in hand
  • Accounts, or games stored on a server
  • Phone layouts and translations

Something leaves this list only through a decision Porter accepts, after real games have been played.
