OpenBattle design · Level 0 · Intent

OpenBattle

What it is for

Two people who own miniatures armies play a full game of their tabletop wargame together online, each in their own browser, with nothing to install.

It feels like the real table

Playing should feel like standing over a real table: a 3D table you look around, models you pick up and set down, dice you throw. Tabletop Simulator mostly gets this feel right. OpenBattle keeps that feel and adds help Tabletop Simulator doesn't give: exact measuring, moving a unit as a unit, a readable history, and undo. As on a real table, each player sees the other moving models, measuring and choosing as it happens (ADR-031).

For whom

Porter and the friends he plays with: people who already know their game's rules and want a shared table.

How we would know it worked

Two players on different networks finish a full game of a OnePageRules wargame (Grimdark Future first) using only OpenBattle for the table, the tape measure and the dice.

What a player can do

  1. Open a new table and get a link to send to the other player.
  2. Join from the link, with a name, and see the same table. Others can join to watch.
  3. Look around the table from any angle.
  4. Place terrain: simple shapes, or 3D meshes loaded from a file, such as scenery exported from Tabletop Simulator.
  5. Place markers that belong to nobody, such as objectives and deployment zones.
  6. Bring an army by typing or pasting a list of units, model counts, base sizes and mesh files, with counters, marks and items such as weapons for their stats, and add more units or models at any point in the game.
  7. Set units aside in reserve and bring them onto the table later.
  8. Pick up and move a model or a whole unit, and see the distance in inches while moving, including along a path with corners, such as around terrain.
  9. Measure between any two points or models, or a model and a marker.
  10. See whether one model can see another, through the true shapes of models and terrain, and what is in the way (ADR-028).
  11. Throw dice that everyone sees land on the same result.
  12. Draw cards from a deck, face up or into your hand, and play or discard them.
  13. Keep track of the round, the phase, counters such as wounds and victory points, and marks such as "has moved", including on terrain, such as "cover". See who the game is waiting on next.
  14. Read every action in a shared log, look back through it, and undo the latest action.
  15. Close the tab, come back later from the same link, and carry on. Save the game to a file.
  16. Play Grimdark Future with its rules package turned on (ADR-029): bring an army from an Army Forge list with its stats, and have the app suggest each step, such as which units can still activate, who can shoot whom, the dice to roll to hit and to block, the models to remove, morale tests, the objectives held at the end of a round, and the winner. Each step is one click, the other player is asked when it is their part, and anything can still be done by hand instead.

Proof from the outside

Every behaviour above is proven by a check that drives the running app the way a person would, in real browsers, and shows the intended result. We don't write unit tests. Something counts as done when its proof passes.

Players apply the rules; a package helps

In the first version the players apply their game's rules and the app never refuses a move. A rules package automates the chores of one game by suggesting each next step to the player whose step it is; it never acts unless a player clicks, and Grimdark Future's is the first (ADR-029). They use the same physical actions a player has, plus the few rules ideas nearly every wargame shares: groups of models and units, phases, priority, counters and marks, and items that carry them. A player can always override a package.

One table, seen the same way

Once everyone's browser has caught up, everyone sees exactly the same table. If two browsers ever disagree, the app says so instead of quietly picking one.

Dice and draws nobody can fix

Nobody, the host included, can choose a dice result or which card is drawn without the others being able to tell.

Games survive

A game survives closing the tab, reloading, and anyone's connection dropping. If the host drops, the game picks up from the host's copy when they return. Any player or spectator can save the game to a file.

Browser to browser

The browsers talk directly. Porter's Craftworld server hands out the app, introduces the browsers to each other, and relays their traffic only when their networks allow nothing else. It never stores a game.

Whose content we ship

OnePageRules names for things may appear in the app and the repository. Nothing from Games Workshop or other publishers ships: no unit names, stats, rules text, art or models. Players bring their own armies. The Grimdark Future package ships the game's mechanics as code and the names of its rules, never OnePageRules' rules text or army book stats; players load their own Army Forge lists (ADR-030).

Not in the first version

  • Rules packages other than Grimdark Future, or loaded from outside the app. Special rules that only an army book defines: the package shows them by name for players to apply.
  • More than two players, or computer opponents
  • Hands that stay secret from someone reading their own browser's data. Hands are hidden on screen only.
  • Accounts, or games stored on a server
  • Phone layouts and translations

Something leaves this list only through a decision Porter accepts.
